# S1 Table. Post-mortem dissection standard operating procedure for *Afruca tangeri*. 

## Purpose
The purpose of tracking deaths and performing post-mortems is to  
1) illuminate any trends, to ensure we are providing the highest standard of crab husbandry and  
2) identify any physiological signs of potentially infectious diseases, including fungal infections, bacterial infections (e.g., shell disease, gill rot, *Vibrio* sp., *Aeromonas* sp. — latter two are zoonotic), parasites and environmental toxins (e.g., TTX — only zoonotic with oral ingestion).  

Any of the above can be imported while introducing crabs to our captive crabitats, who were collected in-situ.  
Fungal infections can arise from overcrowded or poorly maintained crabitats and can cause lesions or discolouration in affected areas.  
Bacterial infections can cause lesions, septicemia or other signs of infection.  
Parasites may include trematodes, nematodes or protozoans, specifically targeting the digestive system and reproductive organs, presenting as inflammation, blockages or other signs of damage.  
Environmental toxins include heavy metals, pesticides, petroleum products, etc., which typically impose respiratory and osmotic stress.

---

## Procedure Background
Post-mortem necropsies are conducted in a systematic manner:  
1) external examination  
2) abdominal flap examination  
3) carapace removal  
4) internal anatomy examination  

Dissection findings are recorded on a separate sheet within the husbandry database; whenever PMs took place the full systematic procedure was completed, so one may assume contents appeared normal unless otherwise stated. A likely cause of death will be provided where evident, but do note this is not a confirmed finding without histological/veterinary support.

---

## Procedure (Step-by-Step Dissections\*)

\*This approach is slightly unorthodox and seeks to examine internal organs by approaching from the dorsal carapace. Some sources go in from the abdomen; typically, this is to ensure ethical crab death by piercing the ventral nerve centre before entering the subcarapacial chamber, but it can be challenging to leave internal organs intact for examination if removing ventral appendages this way. These instructions shall enter the subcarapacial chamber from the dorsal side and therefore please only employ this surgical approach if the dissector is confident the specimen is dead. 


### 1. Put on PPE
Gloves, lab coat, and either a mask or work in a well ventilated area / fume hood.


### 2. External Examination
Assess its overall condition.  
Look for any signs of physical trauma, such as broken or missing limbs, and note the overall coloration and texture of the carapace and appendages.  
Record any missing chelae or pereopods.  
This can provide clues to potential causes of death, such as conspecific aggression or disease.  
Examine empty joints for an epicuticular sheath; absence of a regenerative membrane suggest limb loss was recent.  
While limb loss is not fatal, catastrophic loss or limb loss in combination with other physiological stressors can be.  
Look for holes in the carapace.  
Record any significant findings following standard crab anatomy guidelines (i.e., start pereopod counting at chelae, such as dominant L1) and take photos of significant findings.


### 3. Shell Disease Check
Look for any abnormalities on the carapace.  
If necessary, reference import photographs for this individual.


### 4. Microbial Sample Collection
If any unknown foreign organisms are present (e.g., bacterial & fungal) throughout the post-mortem dissection, collect a sample on a swab and attempt culture for subsequent speciation.  
Culture petri dishes with agar, antibacterial agar, and antifungal agar, as well as marine broth (for suspension) can be located on the ‘crab shelf’ of SWC’s floor 1 east wet lab fridge.  
If possible, swab the sample on prepared 3× agar plates (antifungal Amphotericin B, antibacterial PenStrep, and control) and suspend in marine broth (2216).  
Organisms may grow differently on agar versus in suspension; hence, any suspicious findings should be inoculated on both media.


### 5. Abdominal Flap Examination
Flip the crab upside down so that it’s on its carapace.  
Peel back the abdominal flap and examine the anus, the paired penises or space between the abdominal flap and thoracic sternum.  
Using forceps, put pressure on the anus to record the quantity and coloration of excreta. This suggests whether the crab foraged recently.


### 6. Gnathopod Examination
From external to internal, dissect all mouthparts to evaluate structural integrity:  
- 1× paired mandibles  
- 2× paired maxillae  
- 3× paired maxillipeds  
(12 appendages total)


### 7. Carapace Removal
Flip the crab right side up.  
Using a pair of scissors or a scalpel, slice the bottom left or bottom right corner of the carapace.  
Using a pair of forceps, lift the carapace straight off the body.\*  
Use a scalpel or scissors to peel off the cervical membrane (the flexible, membranous area located between the carapace and the cephalothorax).  

\***Do NOT do this unless you are confident the crab is dead.**


### 8. Internal Organ Examination
Examine the heart, hepatopancreas, upper branchiae and nerve cord.  
Then carefully remove each organ one by one, making an incision to evaluate the health of each organ.


### 9. Gills & Digestive Tract
With the carapace and major internal organs removed, you should now be able to see the crab's branchiae.  
Use a pair of forceps to remove the gills, which are located on either side of the crab's body in the branchial chamber.  
Make a note of gill coloration; often dissections occurring within 12 hrs of death will present pinkish gills.  
Then, remove the digestive tract, which runs along the centre of the crab's body from the mouth to the anus.  
Evaluate the health of the lamellae, digestive tract contents, and hepatopancreas consistency and colour.


### 10. Pathogen & Infection Checks

- **Gill rot check:** look for discoloured or disfigured lamellae.  
- **Aeromonas sp.** (zoonotic): reddening, swelling, or lesions on the exoskeleton or limbs of the crab, discoloured or disfigured lamellae. This species of bacteria can also infect humans, but infection is rare and typically asymptomatic (unless immunologically compromised).  
- **Vibrio sp.** (zoonotic): lesions or ulcers on its exoskeleton, which can become red and inflamed. The crab may also exhibit signs of systemic infection such as swollen joints and loss of limbs. This is incredibly contagious to other crabs and if this is identified, the entire crabitat will need to be treated. This species of bacteria can also infect humans, but infection is rare. Human symptoms include diarrhoea, vomiting, abdominal pain, fever, chills, and in severe cases, blood infections or necrotizing fasciitis ("flesh-eating disease").

_‘Collection techniques for the analyses of pathogens in crustaceans’ is described in Shields (2017)._


### 11. Parasite Check

Look for the relevant parasites.  
If located, consider keeping in ethanol for identification. At minimum, photograph and record.  
Some parasites can also infect humans, again illuminating the importance of PPE. These include:  

- **Trematodes**: a type of flatworm that can infect the intestines or other organs. Trematode infections may not cause symptoms in humans, but heavy infestations can cause abdominal pain, diarrhoea, and other digestive problems.  
- **Nematodes**: a type of roundworm that can infect the intestines, liver, or other organs. Nematode infections may cause symptoms such as abdominal pain, diarrhoea, and weight loss.  
- **Protozoa**: various species, including *Giardia* and *Cryptosporidium*, which can cause gastrointestinal symptoms such as diarrhoea and abdominal cramps in humans.


### 12. Reproductive Organs
With the gills and digestive tract removed, you should now be able to see the reproductive organs of the crab.  

- **Males**: reproductive organs are located in the abdomen, and consist of two testes and a pair of vasa deferentia that lead to the gonopores located on the base of the fifth pair of legs.  
- **Females**: the ovaries are located in the abdomen and are connected to the gonopores by a pair of oviducts. Examine the paired ovaries, oviducts, and genital pores.


### 13. Final Examination
Once all of the internal organs have been examined, take a final look at the external and internal structures of the crab to ensure that nothing has been missed.  
Note any abnormalities or observations that may be relevant to the crab's health or cause of death.

## Post-Procedural Actions
Add PM notes of significant findings, photographs and potentially a cause of death (if one feels confident speculating), to the **Crabitat Seawater Tests** “Post-mortems (necropsies)” sheet.  
If any infectious diseases are suspected, communicate concerns to husbandry staff.
